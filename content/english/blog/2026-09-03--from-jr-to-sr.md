No matter how many years of experience you have, there is always something that haunts us as leaders. We do not wish to micromanage people but we also want to see them do the right things right.

Actually, let's be sincere, we do not want to be *perceived* as micromanagers, and we want to ensure we are not gambling our *accountability* in a reckless way.

Whether you want to see this from the altruist or the selfish angle, both statements are logic traps. If you are already thinking like a leader, _you are not_ a micromanager, _nor you need_ your team to always flawlessly succeed.

I will give you a simple framework that you can apply everyday with the people that report to you, which ensures you are properly balancing _risk and autonomy_, _leaning in and leaning back_, _perception and results_.

Our story today has Tim, our newest hire into our company, who starts asking you many questions about how to do their job. It is not that Tim is not smart, it is just that he is calibrating himself against you, against your expectations, and against your leadership style.

The first thing we must always thrive for is _creating a safe space_ so whenever Tim asks questions, you must never belittle nor give him the impression that his questions or doubts are stupid. Whether Tim asks something appalingly basic or something extremely intricate, your reaction to this should not be to answer, but to *question him back*.

*How would you solve this? What do you think?*

You might think there is a risk that your authority or perception of expertise could get damaged by asking a junior back, but it is indeed the best to do. You are taking this opportunity to create a coaching moment, a learning experience, a proper calibration moment.

You listen to the answer, and here is what you actually do. If Tim knows the right answer, great! Validate it immediately. Do not go about it like *ok do that*, you must actually validate it, introducing your own judgement here. Like we said before, you have to ensure he understands your validation is not simply yourself nudging, regardless of how right he is already.

If you reply back *Ok, good idea, do that Tim, thank you* it could be interpreted that you just took it for what it is. Now, if you say *Tim, that is exactly how we should proceed. Good stuff. If you have any other questions do not hesitate to ask* then its doubly re-assuring. Tim has now levelled up his confidence and you have properly accepted accountability about what Tim is about to do.

However, let's be frank, it is likely that Tim will not hit it right everytime.

Say Tim gives you the wrong answer. Then you challenge him back *There are a couple of reasons why that would not work: A, B and C. However, let's think it through. Think of three other ways this can be done*.

Notice how we did not tell Tim he is wrong or insane, we are listing the reasons why it would not work, which is the new information. Tim might have not known these factors, not thought of considering them. This is yet another coaching moment for you and your reportee. You are calibrating his thought process, teaching him more about the business, and ensuring he is critically thinking stuff.

But do ask him to come back with more than one proposal. Depending on the problem, two or three proposals are good enough.

We then have two scenarios:
* He comes back with N incorrect or incomplete proposals.
* One of his proposals (or more) are actually good.

In the first case, you must ask Tim questions about *that's interesting, what made you think about that?* or *can you help me understanding option B a bit more, tell me about XYZ.*. You know that these proposals are likely incorrect but you should still explore his thought process, to make him take you throught that journey, to ensure he is paying attention and thinking with intense purpose.

Let's say you validated his ideas and yep, you confirm it does not work. After you have shown him this great respect and attention, it is time for you to tell him exactly what to do: *All right, look, these won't work but that's perfectly fine. What I want you do to is XYZ.* This is not micromanagement at all. This is an intentional choice of angle, to take the time and space to calibrate and allow for flow of thought. If you were to fall for the "immediacy trap", to be action biased, to be in a hurry to get things done, then you would have given Tim the right answer from the start. Just, Tim will complete his task accordingly, but now you have taught him two things:
* My manager is there to tell me what to do and how to do it.
* My job is to do what my manager says.

What you might think is a case of *empathy* it is actually a problem of *ruining autonomy*. You are also diluting accountability and you are making your team less scalable. The moment you go are unavailable, everything stops. Even their own growth.

If one of his proposals is good, leave that for the end. Explore the others first, and then, just like at the beginning, _validate it_. Do not just send Tim to do the work, take him through *why* that's how he should do it.

Let's let Tim go home now, he has completed his task flawlessly, happily, and with confidence. We are moving away from the hypothetical examples and into the framework.

## The 3 levels of seniority

I have shown you two of the three situations you can expect with this framework. Let's list them and see why the third one should get you excited.

### Level 1 - Junior

* Situation: Someone comes with a problem, has no idea what to do, and asks you about it.
* Your stance: Do not give them the answer directly. Ask for what could be done. If they have no clue, ask them to think harder and come back to you later. You can timebox this *"come back to me in 1 hour.* or something that ensures this does not become an infinite brainstorming.
* The result: Regardless of whether they find the solution or not, you are helping them calibrate their thought process. You have shown great respect for their ideas, while at the same time showing them why they report to you.

### Level 2 - Intermediate

* Situation: Someone comes with a problem, and they already come with solutions. They ask you which one to go for.
* Your stance: Fantastic, they are learning that you will always have them think, so they think! Obvious problems are solved automatically without your involvement, and everytime they identify something else, they get you involved willingly and happily, without being worried that you will see this as a performance issue.
* The result: Same as with Level 1, you take them through the options, weight pros and cons, and either choose one or give him the answer. 

### Level 3 - Senior

* Situation: Someone comes with a problem, they thought of 3 solutions, and they applied the one you would chosen.
* Your stance: As you are coaching your teammmeber in Level 2, eventually you should let him know you _expect him_ to build up the confidence to do this on their own, while at the same time you continue to remain _accountable_ for their decisions. You both have calibrated with each other among numerous interactions and and are thinking alike. You have built their _autonomy_ without relinquishing your own position.
* The result: This person is now you. They are an extension of you. You both now truly work as a unit.

## People are Junior and Senior at the same time

These levels are not something you apply to _the person_ but to their _skills_. They can be level 3 at design but level 1 at writing content, level 3 at coding but level 1 at automating quality, level 3 at coaching others but level 1 at understanding the business politics.

It is not necessary to keep a profile dossier of each employee that reports to you and write down the levels. This is not something you should turn into a faceless, empathy-less, bureaucratic form that you now fill.

People are always concerned about their performance. But what this truly means is how their manager perceives their performance to be. Much conflict arises when these two views do not overlap, when a person's view on their performance completely misaligns with their managers', especially with long-tenure folks.

## The risks of this system

If you introduce the concept too early to your folks, they will start coming with the 3 proposals all the time (overthinking) or they will jump the gun too often (gamgling so they can be perceived level 3 to you).
